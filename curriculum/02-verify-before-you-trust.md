# Module 2 — Verify Before You Trust

**Estimated time:** 12–15 minutes  
**E3RC principle:** VERIFY  
**FIRST connection:** Discovery, high-quality work, Gracious Professionalism®

## Learning goals

By the end of this module, a student should be able to:

- explain why an AI answer can sound certain and still be wrong;
- distinguish an AI response from an authoritative source;
- use a practical verification process for robotics, schoolwork, and personal decisions;
- recognize fabricated citations, invented rule numbers, outdated information, and missing context.

## AI does not “know” in the same way you do

Generative AI predicts useful responses from patterns in data. It can produce excellent explanations, code, summaries, and ideas, but fluency is not proof of truth. A model can produce a detailed answer with a confident tone, exact-looking numbers, a realistic citation, or even a rule number that does not exist.

This problem is often called a **hallucination** or **confabulation**. The important lesson is not the label. The important lesson is that **confidence and detail are not evidence**.

AI can also be wrong because information has changed. This matters especially in FRC, where game rules, software, legal parts, inspection requirements, APIs, vendor libraries, and control-system documentation can change between seasons or during a season.

## The authority ladder

When an answer matters, ask: **What source has authority over this question?**

For Team 1555, a useful order is:

1. **Current official source** — current FRC manual, official FIRST documentation, current vendor manual, teacher instructions, school policy, law/regulation, or primary source.
2. **Reliable secondary source** — a respected technical reference, textbook, reputable journalism, or well-maintained community documentation.
3. **Community experience** — forums, Chief Delphi, Reddit, Discord, videos, teammates, mentors.
4. **AI-generated answer** — useful for finding directions, explaining concepts, generating hypotheses, or helping locate sources, but not automatically authoritative.

Community and AI sources can be extremely useful. They simply require verification when the stakes are meaningful.

## Team 1555 scenario: the imaginary rule

You ask an AI whether a particular motor controller is legal on the current FRC robot. It responds:

> “Yes. Rule R712 specifically permits this controller.”

That sounds strong. It is also exactly the kind of answer that must be checked.

The right workflow is:

1. Open the **current official game manual**.
2. Search for the device, category, and cited rule.
3. Check current FIRST updates or team updates if relevant.
4. Check the manufacturer documentation if the question concerns electrical limits or configuration.
5. If there is still ambiguity, ask a mentor, inspector, or other appropriate human — and clearly state what is verified versus uncertain.

Do **not** make the robot legal by asking the AI three times until it agrees with itself.

## Schoolwork scenario: the perfect citation

An AI gives you a quotation, author, journal title, publication date, and DOI for a research paper. Before citing it:

- search for the paper in the library, journal, DOI resolver, or another reliable source;
- confirm the title, authors, date, and actual claim;
- read enough of the original source to know that it supports your statement.

A fake citation can look almost indistinguishable from a real one.

## Personal-life scenario: “everyone is reporting this”

You see a screenshot or post claiming a major event happened. An AI summarizes the claim and provides details. Before sharing it:

- identify the original source;
- check whether multiple **independent** reputable sources confirm it;
- look for the actual statement, document, video, or event being referenced;
- check the date and context;
- distinguish reporting from reposting.

Ten accounts repeating the same unsupported post are not ten independent sources.

## The 5-question verification check

Before acting on an important AI answer, ask:

1. **What would happen if this were wrong?**
2. **What is the authoritative source?**
3. **Can I verify the key claim independently?**
4. **Is the information current?**
5. **Am I presenting uncertainty honestly?**

The higher the consequence — robot damage, injury, grades, money, reputation, privacy — the stronger the verification should be.

## Key takeaway

> **AI can help you find an answer. Verification is how you decide whether to trust it.**
