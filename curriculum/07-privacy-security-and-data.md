# Module 7 — Privacy, Security, and Data

**Estimated time:** 12–15 minutes  
**E3RC principles:** PROTECT, OWN IT  
**FIRST connection:** Safety, respect, Gracious Professionalism®

## Learning goals

Students should be able to:

- recognize sensitive information before sharing it with an AI tool;
- explain why “private” and “secure” are not the same thing;
- protect credentials, student data, personal images, and confidential team information;
- apply basic cybersecurity habits to AI use.

## Every prompt can contain data

When you type into an AI system, upload a file, share a photo, paste code, or connect an account, you may be sending information to another service.

Before you do that, ask:

> **Does this tool actually need this information to help me?**

If the answer is no, do not share it.

## Never casually paste secrets into AI

Examples include:

- passwords;
- API keys;
- access tokens;
- Wi-Fi credentials;
- private GitHub repositories;
- personal student information;
- private medical or financial information;
- unreleased sponsor/customer information;
- documents you do not have permission to upload.

A programming assistant may be excellent at debugging code, but that does not mean it needs your production credentials.

## Team 1555 scenario: the “helpful” repository upload

A student wants an AI coding assistant to debug a project and pastes an entire private repository into a public chatbot. The repository includes a secret token and student names.

Even if the student meant well, this creates unnecessary exposure.

A better process:

1. Remove secrets and credentials.
2. Share the smallest relevant code sample.
3. Remove personal data unless it is truly necessary.
4. Check whether the tool is approved for the kind of data involved.
5. Rotate any credential that was accidentally exposed.

## Student privacy matters

Federal education privacy guidance warns schools to review online tools before sharing personally identifiable information from education records. Even when E3RC is not acting as a student's public school, the principle is still useful: **collect and disclose only what you need, and know where the data is going.**

For this training platform, E3RC should collect as little as practical: student name, completion status, score, certificate ID, and optional email only when needed for delivery.

## Photos, voices, and faces are data too

Students often think of privacy as passwords and account numbers. But biometric and personal media can also be sensitive.

Before uploading someone else’s photo or voice to an AI system, consider:

- Did they agree to this use?
- Could the output embarrass or harm them?
- Will the service retain the media?
- Does the output imitate them or create something they never said or did?

## The Core 4

The U.S. Department of Education’s 2026 K–12 cybersecurity guidance recommends four basic habits:

1. Use long, unique passwords — ideally with a password manager.
2. Enable multi-factor authentication when available.
3. Keep devices, software, and apps updated.
4. Stay alert for scams in email, text messages, and direct messages.

These habits matter even more when AI makes phishing and impersonation easier to create.

## Privacy vs convenience

AI tools often ask for access to files, contacts, photos, microphones, calendars, or cloud drives. Convenience is not automatically worth the access.

Use the minimum permissions needed. Review connected apps occasionally. Remove access you no longer need.

## Key takeaway

> **If information is private, sensitive, secret, or belongs to someone else, stop before you upload it.**
