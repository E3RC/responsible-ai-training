# Module 3 — Schoolwork, Learning, and Academic Integrity

**Estimated time:** 12–15 minutes  
**E3RC principles:** DISCLOSE, OWN IT  
**FIRST connection:** Discovery, Teamwork, Gracious Professionalism®

## Learning goals

Students should be able to:

- separate AI-assisted learning from AI-substituted work;
- follow the specific rules of an assignment or teacher;
- disclose AI assistance when required or appropriate;
- use AI in ways that strengthen rather than replace learning.

## AI use is not automatically cheating — but rules matter

AI can be a useful tutor, editor, quiz generator, coding explainer, translation aid, brainstorming partner, and accessibility tool. It can also do work a teacher expected the student to do.

The key rule is simple:

> **The person assigning the work gets to define what assistance is allowed.**

A use that is appropriate in one class may be prohibited in another. A teacher may allow brainstorming but not drafting, allow grammar feedback but not rewriting, or ban AI entirely on a particular assignment.

## The “learning test”

Before using AI, ask:

- Am I still doing the thinking this assignment is meant to measure?
- Could I explain the final work without the AI beside me?
- Did I verify factual claims and citations?
- Would I be comfortable telling my teacher exactly how I used AI?

If the honest answer to the last question is “no,” that is a warning sign.

## Examples

### Usually constructive uses, when allowed

- “Explain this physics concept in a different way.”
- “Quiz me on these notes without giving me the answer immediately.”
- “Give me feedback on where my argument is unclear.”
- “Show me three approaches to solving this programming problem, but do not write the final code.”
- “Help me make a study plan for Friday’s test.”

### Often inappropriate unless specifically permitted

- Submitting AI-generated writing as your own work.
- Asking AI to solve an assessment that is meant to measure your own skill.
- Inventing sources, data, quotations, lab results, or experiences.
- Using AI after a teacher explicitly said not to.
- Hiding substantial AI assistance when disclosure is required.

## Team 1555 connection

FRC students routinely cross between schoolwork and team work. A student may use the same AI coding assistant for AP Computer Science, a personal project, and robot code — but the rules can be different in each place.

At E3RC, the goal is not to pretend AI does not exist. The goal is to develop students who can use advanced tools **without surrendering ownership of their learning or work**.

## A practical disclosure format

When disclosure is appropriate, it does not need to be dramatic. For example:

> “I used an AI assistant to brainstorm possible outlines and identify unclear sentences. I wrote the final response and verified the cited information myself.”

For code:

> “An AI coding assistant suggested the initial structure for this method. I reviewed, modified, tested, and can explain the final implementation.”

The exact disclosure standard should follow the teacher’s or organization’s rules.

## AI detectors are not proof

Students should also understand that automated “AI detectors” can make mistakes. A detection score is not the same as evidence of misconduct. Ethical use should be judged from the actual rules, process, work, and evidence — not from a single opaque score.

## Current education context

The U.S. Department of Education’s 2025–2026 AI guidance encourages AI literacy and responsible use rather than treating all AI as forbidden. Indiana’s current computer science guidance similarly emphasizes preparing students to be critical consumers and responsible creators of AI-enabled technology.

## Key takeaway

> **Use AI to increase your capability, not to fake capability you have not developed.**
