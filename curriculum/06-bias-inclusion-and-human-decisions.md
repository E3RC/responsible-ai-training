# Module 6 — Bias, Inclusion, and Human Decisions

**Estimated time:** 12–15 minutes  
**E3RC principles:** VERIFY, RESPECT, OWN IT  
**FIRST connection:** Inclusion, Impact, Teamwork

## Learning goals

Students should be able to:

- explain how bias can enter AI systems through data, design, labels, and deployment;
- identify when an automated recommendation should not replace accountable human judgment;
- recognize fairness and inclusion issues in robotics, school, and everyday uses of AI;
- ask better questions about who benefits, who may be harmed, and who can appeal a decision.

## Computers are not automatically objective

An AI system is built from human choices: what data to collect, what examples to include, what goal to optimize, what labels to use, and where the tool is deployed. Bias can enter at any of those points.

A model might perform better for some groups than others. A ranking system might use a proxy that disadvantages certain people. An AI-generated image tool might reproduce stereotypes. A recommendation engine can amplify patterns already present in historical data.

This does not mean every AI system is unfair. It means **we have to test rather than assume fairness**.

## Scenario: scholarship screening

Imagine E3RC has 100 scholarship essays and considers using AI to rank them.

Questions to ask:

- What information is the model seeing?
- Is any protected or irrelevant personal information included?
- What criteria determine a “good” essay?
- Could writing style be confused with merit?
- Does the system disadvantage students who write differently or use English differently?
- Can a human review and override the recommendation?
- Can a student understand or appeal the decision?

For a high-impact decision, AI can assist, but the accountable human decision-maker should remain visible.

## FRC scenario: scouting and strategy

Suppose an AI scouting system ranks teams using match data. If the data is incomplete, noisy, or biased toward certain metrics, the ranking may look precise while hiding bad assumptions.

A responsible team asks:

- Which metrics matter?
- What data is missing?
- Is the system overvaluing scoring while ignoring defense or reliability?
- Does it unfairly penalize teams that had temporary mechanical failures?
- Can human scouts explain why a team was ranked where it was?

This is both an engineering and an ethics lesson: **models optimize what you tell them to measure, not necessarily what you truly care about.**

## Personal-life scenario: appearance and stereotypes

Prompting AI to “make this person look more professional” may produce different changes depending on perceived gender, race, age, disability, or culture. That can reveal stereotypes embedded in data or design choices.

Students should learn to notice when “normal,” “professional,” “attractive,” “dangerous,” or “successful” are being treated as if they were objective categories.

## Human oversight is not just a checkbox

Keeping a human “in the loop” only helps if that human:

- has enough information to challenge the AI;
- has the authority to override it;
- understands the limitations of the system;
- has time to review important cases;
- is accountable for the final decision.

## FIRST connection

FIRST’s current Core Values explicitly include **Inclusion** — respecting one another and embracing differences — and **Impact** — applying what we learn to improve the world. Responsible AI use should support both.

## Key takeaway

> **An automated answer can be consistent without being fair. Ask what the system is optimizing, who it affects, and who remains accountable.**
