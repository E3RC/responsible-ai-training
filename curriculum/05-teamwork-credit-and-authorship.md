# Module 5 — Teamwork, Credit, and Authorship

**Estimated time:** 12–15 minutes  
**E3RC principles:** DISCLOSE, RESPECT, OWN IT  
**FIRST connection:** Teamwork, Inclusion, Gracious Professionalism®

## Learning goals

Students should be able to:

- distinguish assistance from authorship;
- give appropriate credit for human and AI contributions;
- avoid inventing or exaggerating team accomplishments;
- use AI in award submissions and team communications without losing team ownership.

## AI can help a team communicate — but it cannot create accomplishments that never happened

Teams write a lot: award submissions, sponsor letters, social posts, build logs, technical documentation, outreach reports, presentations, and student nominations. AI can help organize, edit, summarize, and improve clarity.

The ethical line is not “AI touched the document.” The important questions are:

- Are the facts true?
- Does the document still represent the team’s actual work and voice?
- Did the team review the final result?
- Were rules about authorship or disclosure followed?
- Are human contributors receiving appropriate credit?

## Scenario: the amazing award essay

A student feeds a few bullet points into an AI system. The AI returns a polished essay describing dozens of outreach events, measurable impact, and partnerships. Some of those details are plausible but were never provided by the team.

The essay sounds impressive. It is also unacceptable.

AI-generated fabrication is still fabrication.

For FIRST-related submissions, Team 1555 should be able to support claims with real examples, dates, results, records, photos, metrics, or team knowledge. A good tool may help explain an accomplishment. It must not manufacture one.

## Team authorship

A team submission should not become one student plus a chatbot speaking on behalf of everyone else.

A healthy workflow is:

1. Collect real information from the people who did the work.
2. Draft from those facts and experiences.
3. Use AI for organization, alternatives, clarity, editing, or critique when useful.
4. Fact-check the output.
5. Have the relevant team members review it.
6. Follow any applicable FIRST or school submission rules.

## Credit inside technical work

Suppose a programmer uses an AI assistant to suggest a path-planning algorithm and then spends hours adapting and testing it. Saying “AI wrote everything” ignores the student’s engineering work. Saying “I invented the entire method from scratch” can also be misleading.

A more accurate description is often:

> “I used an AI coding assistant to explore approaches, then implemented, reviewed, modified, and tested the final solution.”

Good disclosure is about accuracy, not embarrassment.

## Respect the work of other people

AI does not erase the need to respect creators. Students should not:

- remove someone’s name and present their work as their own;
- use AI to imitate a teammate, teacher, sponsor, artist, or public figure deceptively;
- upload private work to an external system without permission;
- ask AI to rewrite copied material merely to hide plagiarism.

## FIRST connection

FIRST describes Gracious Professionalism as high-quality work that values others and respects individuals and the community. That fits AI-assisted teamwork directly: use powerful tools while remaining truthful, collaborative, and respectful.

## Key takeaway

> **AI can polish the story. The story still has to be yours — and true.**
