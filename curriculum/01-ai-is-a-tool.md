# Module 1 — AI Is a Tool, Not an Authority

**Target time:** 12 minutes  
**Primary rules:** VERIFY, OWN IT  
**FIRST connections:** Discovery, Innovation, Gracious Professionalism

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module, students should be able to:

- describe generative AI as a prediction system rather than an all-knowing source;
- explain why an AI response can sound confident and still be wrong;
- distinguish useful assistance from transferred responsibility;
- identify situations where human review is required before acting on AI output.

## Read: What an AI assistant is actually doing

Modern AI assistants are very good at producing useful language, code, images, explanations, summaries, and ideas. They can help you learn faster, compare approaches, debug code, brainstorm designs, or turn a rough thought into something more organized.

But an AI assistant does not "know" facts in the same way a person checks a current manual, measures a part, runs a test, or observes the physical world. It generates responses from patterns learned from large amounts of data and from the information available in the current conversation or connected tools.

That distinction matters because AI can produce an answer that is:

- fluent;
- specific;
- technically formatted;
- confident;
- and completely wrong.

This kind of fabricated or unsupported output is often called a **hallucination**.

A made-up answer can be especially dangerous when it includes details that look authoritative, such as a rule number, citation, datasheet value, wiring instruction, command, or safety recommendation.

## Team 1555 scenario

You are building a new mechanism and ask an AI assistant which breaker, wire size, and motor controller configuration to use. The answer looks professional and includes values that sound familiar.

What has the AI actually given you?

It has given you a **candidate answer to investigate**. It has not given you permission to skip the current FRC rules, vendor documentation, electrical standards, mentor review, or testing.

If the answer affects a robot, a person, electrical equipment, safety, competition legality, or expensive hardware, the standard for verification should be higher.

## AI should expand your ability, not replace your responsibility

Using AI is not automatically irresponsible. On Team 1555, it can be a powerful engineering tool.

Examples of productive uses include:

- asking for several approaches to a programming problem;
- having AI explain unfamiliar code line by line;
- generating test cases;
- brainstorming mechanism concepts;
- summarizing documentation you then verify;
- identifying questions you should investigate;
- practicing interview or judging questions;
- helping make technical writing clearer.

The problem begins when a student treats AI output as if responsibility moved from the student to the tool.

If you submit it, publish it, wire it, build it, deploy it, or tell another team to rely on it, you still own the result.

## School and personal-life connection

The same rule applies outside robotics.

If AI gives you a historical claim for an essay, you are responsible for checking it. If it drafts a social-media post accusing someone of something, you are responsible for the consequences of publishing it. If it gives you instructions for a risky activity, the words "the AI told me to" do not remove your responsibility to use judgment.

## The first two rules

### VERIFY

AI output is not automatically true. Important claims should be checked against reliable sources, current documentation, measurements, tests, or qualified people.

### OWN IT

You remain responsible for what you submit, publish, build, deploy, or recommend.

## Quick scenario

An AI assistant generates autonomous code that compiles on the first try. Does that mean the code is correct?

No. Compilation only shows that the code met certain syntax and build requirements. It does not prove that the code behaves as intended, handles failures safely, follows current FRC rules, or will work correctly on the physical robot.

## Key takeaways

- AI is a powerful tool, not an authority.
- Confidence and detail are not proof of accuracy.
- Treat important AI output as something to verify.
- Human responsibility does not disappear because AI helped create the work.
- In engineering, physical safety and competition legality require especially careful review.
